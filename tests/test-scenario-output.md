### DOMAIN ASSESSMENT -- Governance, Stakeholder and Institutional Leadership Agent

#### PROBLEM FRAMING

This question sits at the absolute center of my jurisdiction. A political sponsor is not a stakeholder among many -- the sponsor is the programme's institutional anchor, the source of its political authorization, and often the keystone of the supporting coalition. Sponsor loss is not a personnel change; it is a governance architecture event that threatens the legitimacy, authority, and institutional viability of the entire programme. I am assessing whether the programme's governance arrangements can survive the removal of their political foundation.

#### BOTTOM-LINE POSITION

The loss of a political sponsor before a gate review is a **governance emergency**. Unless the programme has already constructed a broad-based coalition with distributed political support, sponsor departure will create a legitimacy vacuum, expose latent stakeholder fractures, and leave the programme without the upward authority it needs to pass through the gate. Programmes that depend on a single sponsor relationship are structurally fragile, and this fragility is about to be tested. The gate review itself becomes a crisis point: without sponsor advocacy, the programme faces the gate naked.

#### KEY FINDINGS

1. **Sponsor loss removes the programme's political authorization and upward cover.**
   The SRO's ability to secure resources, resolve inter-institutional disputes, and protect the programme from political interference depends on the sponsor's continued support. Without the sponsor, the SRO is politically exposed.
   Evidence: doctrine (Module 3 -- SRO role, managing upwards, sponsor relationships) | Strength: strong

2. **Coalition brittleness will be exposed immediately.**
   If the supporting coalition was held together primarily by the departing sponsor's personal authority or political position, the coalition will fragment. Members whose support was conditional on the sponsor's presence will reassess. Blocking coalitions that were restrained by the sponsor may activate.
   Evidence: doctrine (Sebenius on coalition sequencing, Susskind on winning/blocking coalitions) | Strength: strong

3. **Governance authority becomes ambiguous.**
   The departing sponsor likely held formal or informal authority over key governance decisions -- board appointments, funding approvals, scope arbitration. Their departure creates a governance vacuum where decision rights are contested or unclaimed. This is not an administrative gap; it is a structural inability to act.
   Evidence: doctrine (governance architecture design, decision rights allocation) | Strength: strong

4. **Legitimacy erosion begins immediately and accelerates.**
   The programme's legitimacy with other institutional stakeholders, the public, and the delivery supply chain is partly derived from its sponsor's credibility. Sponsor departure signals political uncertainty, which invites scrutiny, opportunistic challenge, and confidence withdrawal.
   Evidence: doctrine (legitimacy as operating condition, social license), case_analogy | Strength: moderate

5. **Assurance processes may be captured or redirected by a new political agenda.**
   A successor sponsor -- or the absence of one -- may restructure assurance arrangements to serve different political purposes. Gateway review findings may be reinterpreted through a new political lens. The independence of assurance is threatened.
   Evidence: inferred_judgment | Strength: weak

#### CRITICAL RISKS

1. **Governance vacuum at gate review** -- The programme enters the gate without a political advocate. Severity: critical. Likelihood: near-certain if no succession plan exists.

2. **Coalition fragmentation** -- Supporting stakeholders defect or become passive. Severity: high. Likelihood: likely if the coalition was sponsor-dependent.

3. **Blocking coalition activation** -- Opponents previously restrained by the sponsor's authority exploit the vacuum. Severity: high. Likelihood: possible.

4. **Institutional deauthorization** -- The programme loses its institutional mandate or has its scope, funding, or authority reduced by successor leadership. Severity: critical. Likelihood: possible.

#### OPPORTUNITIES

- Sponsor transition can be an opportunity to broaden the coalition base, reducing future single-point-of-failure risk in political support.
- A new sponsor may bring fresh political capital and potentially stronger alignment with current policy priorities.

#### ASSUMPTIONS

- behavioral_assumption: Other coalition members' support is at least partly contingent on the sponsor's continued advocacy.
- stakeholder_assumption: Blocking coalitions or latent opponents exist who are currently restrained by the sponsor's authority.
- temporal_assumption: There is insufficient time between sponsor departure and gate review to construct a full replacement governance arrangement.
- evidence_assumption: The programme has not already implemented a sponsor succession or coalition broadening strategy.

### DOMAIN ASSESSMENT -- Risk, Uncertainty and Fragility Agent

#### PROBLEM FRAMING

I see this question through the lens of downside exposure and concealed variance. The departure of a political sponsor is not a "risk event" in the register sense -- it is a structural shift in the programme's operating environment that changes the distribution of all outcomes. Every estimate, every assumption, every forecast that rested on continued political support must now be re-examined. I am assessing what the outside view tells us about programmes that lose their political sponsors, how fat the tails of the downside distribution become, and whether the programme has any contingency architecture for this scenario.

#### BOTTOM-LINE POSITION

Sponsor loss is a **tail-risk crystallization event**. It does not merely add a risk to the register; it transforms the programme's entire risk profile. The base rate for major programmes that lose political sponsors is grim: the reference class includes cancellation, indefinite deferral, radical descoping, and politically motivated restructuring. The programme's current risk management system -- if it treated continued political support as a background assumption rather than a monitored risk -- has failed to anticipate a foreseeable disruption. The variance around every programme outcome has just widened dramatically.

#### KEY FINDINGS

1. **Reference class data for sponsor-loss scenarios shows severe programme disruption.**
   The outside view is stark: major programmes that lose political sponsors during delivery exhibit high rates of cancellation, descoping, budget cuts, and strategic redirection. Flyvbjerg's iron law applies with particular force: sponsor loss removes the political protection that shielded the programme from the normal pressures of scrutiny, reallocation, and political reprioritization.
   Evidence: empirical_research (Flyvbjerg on political drivers of megaproject outcomes), case_analogy | Strength: moderate

2. **The programme's risk profile has changed structurally, not incrementally.**
   This is not one more risk to add to the register. Sponsor loss changes the conditional probability of multiple existing risks simultaneously. Funding risk, scope risk, schedule risk, governance risk, and legitimacy risk are all correlated with political support. Losing the sponsor shifts the entire distribution, not a single variable.
   Evidence: doctrine (systems risk, uncertainty classes) | Strength: strong

3. **Escalation of commitment dynamics may emerge in the successor's response.**
   A new political principal may face a classic escalation dilemma: continue a programme they did not authorize (absorbing political risk for someone else's commitments) or terminate it (absorbing the political and financial cost of cancellation). The incentive structure often pushes toward slow strangulation -- continuing the programme nominally while withdrawing real resources and attention.
   Evidence: doctrine (Drummond on escalation of commitment), case_analogy | Strength: moderate

4. **The programme likely treated continued political support as a background assumption, not a monitored risk.**
   If sponsor loss was not explicitly on the risk register with contingency plans, the programme has been operating with a blind spot on one of its most consequential dependencies. This is itself a failure of risk intelligence.
   Evidence: inferred_judgment (based on typical programme risk management practice) | Strength: weak

5. **Variance around cost, schedule, and benefits estimates has expanded.**
   Every forecast that assumed continued political support must now carry wider uncertainty bands. The "expected case" in the business case may no longer be the most likely outcome. P90 scenarios should now include politically-driven descoping or termination.
   Evidence: doctrine (distributional thinking, reference class forecasting) | Strength: moderate

#### CRITICAL RISKS

1. **Programme cancellation or indefinite deferral** -- Successor decides the programme is not a political priority. Severity: critical. Likelihood: possible (depends on successor's agenda and sunk cost calculus).

2. **Death by a thousand cuts** -- Programme continues nominally but loses real resources, political protection, and institutional priority. Slow degradation rather than clean termination. Severity: high. Likelihood: likely if no strong replacement sponsor emerges.

3. **Escalation trap** -- Programme becomes politically toxic but too expensive to cancel, trapping successor in a commitment spiral. Severity: high. Likelihood: possible.

4. **Risk management system failure** -- The programme's risk system did not anticipate this scenario, raising the question of what else it has failed to anticipate. Severity: moderate. Likelihood: likely.

#### OPPORTUNITIES

- A forced reassessment of the business case may reveal scope reduction or redesign options that actually improve the programme's value proposition.
- If the programme was already exhibiting escalation dynamics under the original sponsor, sponsor departure may provide a politically acceptable exit ramp.

#### ASSUMPTIONS

- evidence_assumption: Reference class data from comparable programmes with sponsor loss is applicable to this programme's sector and context.
- behavioral_assumption: The successor political leadership will not inherit the departing sponsor's commitment to the programme without their own independent reasons for support.
- structural_assumption: The programme does not have existing contingency plans or succession arrangements for sponsor loss.
- futures_assumption: The political environment is such that the programme is not automatically protected by broader policy commitments or institutional mandates that transcend individual sponsors.

#### DEPENDENCIES

This assessment depends on the Governance Agent's analysis of coalition structure (is the sponsor truly a single point of failure?). It depends on the Strategic Leadership Agent's assessment of whether programme leadership can independently sustain political support. It depends on the Temporal Dynamics Agent's analysis of whether there is time to rebuild support before the gate.

#### CROSS-DOMAIN IMPLICATIONS

- **Governance, Stakeholder and Institutional Leadership Agent** should note: the risk profile change means governance arrangements must be assessed not for normal operations but for crisis conditions.
- **Strategic Leadership and Performance Agent** should note: leadership's ability to manage upward under sponsor vacuum is now the most consequential variable in the programme's risk profile.
- **Cascading Effects Agent** should note: this is a high-severity trigger with multiple propagation pathways. The cascade analysis should treat this as a potentially non-linear amplification scenario, not a proportional transmission.
- **Futures and Foresight Agent** should note: the programme's future option space has contracted. Lock-in decisions that were viable under political protection may become liabilities without it.

#### CONFIDENCE

Level: moderate

- Evidence quality: moderate (reference class data exists but is not programme-specific)
- Evidence relevance: analogous (comparable programmes, not direct evidence about this programme)
- Assumption stability: uncertain (behavioral assumptions about successor response are speculative)
- Cross-domain completeness: partial

#### CONDITIONS FOR VALIDITY

This assessment holds if the programme is operating in a political environment where individual sponsors have material influence over programme fate. In institutional environments where programmes are protected by strong legal mandates, broad cross-party support, or contractual commitments that survive political transitions, the severity is reduced. ### DOMAIN ASSESSMENT -- Strategic Leadership and Performance Agent

#### PROBLEM FRAMING

I see this through the lens of decision culture, political dynamics, and leadership capability under pressure. Sponsor loss is not merely a governance event -- it is a political earthquake that tests the entire decision-making system. Who actually held power? Whose support was genuine and whose was performative? What happens to the information flows, the reporting chain, the political cover that allowed the SRO to operate? I am diagnosing the programme's leadership system under conditions of sudden political exposure.

#### BOTTOM-LINE POSITION

The most immediate consequence of sponsor loss is **leadership exposure**. The SRO and programme director have been operating within a political protection structure -- possibly without fully recognizing how much their effectiveness depended on the sponsor's authority rather than their own. That protection has been removed. Their decisions, their performance, their very strategic direction are now politically unprotected. The critical question is whether the programme's leadership has the relational capital, political skill, and network position to survive and reestablish support independently -- or whether they were dependent on the sponsor's political infrastructure in ways that will now become painfully apparent.

#### KEY FINDINGS

1. **The SRO's effective authority was likely derived from the sponsor's political position, not from the SRO's own institutional standing.**
   In most major programme contexts, the SRO's ability to convene, to resolve disputes, to secure resources, and to protect the programme from interference is contingent on sponsor backing. Without that backing, the SRO's positional authority remains but their effective power diminishes sharply.
   Evidence: doctrine (Module 6 -- influence without authority, managing upwards; Module 3 -- SRO role), practitioner_framework | Strength: strong

2. **Decision culture will shift from proactive to defensive.**
   Under political uncertainty, programme leadership will predictably shift to risk-averse, defensive decision-making. Bold commitments will be deferred. Innovation will be suppressed. The programme will enter a holding pattern -- precisely when active leadership is most needed to navigate the transition.
   Evidence: doctrine (adaptive leadership, decision quality under uncertainty), inferred_judgment | Strength: moderate

3. **Information flows will be disrupted and politicized.**
   The departure of the sponsor changes who needs to be informed, what information is politically safe to share, and how performance is reported. Programme leadership may begin filtering information to protect the programme's political viability rather than to support sound decision-making. This is not dishonesty -- it is a rational response to political exposure that nevertheless degrades decision quality.
   Evidence: doctrine (political capture of information flows, Goodhart's Law), inferred_judgment | Strength: moderate

4. **Performance metrics will be scrutinized through a new political lens.**
   A successor sponsor or political principal may apply different performance criteria, different value weightings, or different evaluation timescales. What counted as success under the previous sponsor may not count as success under the new political regime. The programme's performance narrative may need to be reconstructed.
   Evidence: doctrine (performance measurement distortion, evaluating in time), case_analogy | Strength: moderate

5. **Informal influence networks that depended on the sponsor will collapse.**
   The sponsor's departure does not only remove one person -- it removes that person's network, their allies, their institutional relationships, and the informal channels through which the programme accessed political intelligence, secured inter-agency cooperation, and managed upward. This network loss may be more consequential than the formal sponsor role.
   Evidence: doctrine (Ibarra on networks, Uzzi and Dunlap on strategic networks), inferred_judgment | Strength: moderate

#### CRITICAL RISKS

1. **Leadership paralysis** -- SRO and programme team freeze under political uncertainty, deferring critical decisions. Severity: high. Likelihood: likely.

2. **Political capture of reporting** -- Information flows become distorted as leadership manages perceptions rather than performance. Severity: moderate. Likelihood: likely.

3. **Strategy-execution disconnect** -- Strategic direction set by the departing sponsor is no longer politically supported, but the programme continues executing against an obsolete mandate. Severity: high. Likelihood: possible.

4. **Successor imposes new performance criteria** -- The programme is judged against criteria it was not designed to meet. Severity: moderate. Likelihood: possible.

#### OPPORTUNITIES

- Political transition can be a catalyst for honest performance reassessment -- clearing away performative optimism that served the previous sponsor's political needs.
- New leadership may bring mandate to reform or refocus the programme in ways that improve its actual value proposition.

#### ASSUMPTIONS

- behavioral_assumption: The SRO's effectiveness is significantly contingent on sponsor backing, not solely on their own institutional authority and political skill.
- structural_assumption: The programme's decision-making processes are structured around upward reporting to the sponsor's office.
- stakeholder_assumption: The programme's informal influence networks are substantially connected through the departing sponsor's political apparatus.
- temporal_assumption: The leadership team has not pre-positioned relationships with potential successor sponsors.

#### DEPENDENCIES

This assessment depends on the Governance Agent confirming whether the programme's formal governance can function independently of the sponsor. It depends on the Risk Agent assessing the probability of various successor scenarios. It depends on the Temporal Dynamics Agent assessing whether there is time for leadership to rebuild political infrastructure before the gate.

- **Governance, Stakeholder and Institutional Leadership Agent** should note: governance architecture may be formally intact but effectively hollow without sponsor authority animating it. The gap between formal governance and actual decision-making will widen.
- **Risk, Uncertainty and Fragility Agent** should note: leadership defensive posture will suppress honest risk reporting precisely when accurate risk intelligence is most critical.

I see this question as an exploration of how a political discontinuity reshapes the programme's future option space. Sponsor loss is not merely something that happened -- it is a signal that the political environment in which the programme operates is less stable than assumed. Every programme plan, every strategic assumption, every governance arrangement that rested on the implicit assumption "our political environment will remain stable throughout delivery" has just been invalidated. The immediate crisis is real, but the deeper lesson is that the programme was almost certainly planning for a single political future -- the continuation of current conditions -- and has been caught by a discontinuity that should have been within its scenario space all along. The programme's future option space has narrowed: decisions that were viable under political protection may now create dangerous lock-in, and the programme's strategic flexibility has been reduced at exactly the moment when flexibility is most needed.

#### KEY FINDINGS

1. **The programme was operating under a presentist assumption about political stability.**
   Treating continued sponsor support as a background condition rather than a scenario variable is a classic presentist failure. Major programmes with multi-year delivery timelines will almost certainly span at least one political transition. This should have been a scenario in the programme's planning, not a surprise.
   Evidence: doctrine (presentism as cognitive bias, Module 8), practitioner_framework | Strength: strong

   Decisions that were viable under political protection -- ambitious scope, transformative design, controversial procurement strategies -- may no longer be sustainable. The programme's option space has narrowed because political cover for bold choices has been withdrawn.

   Contractual commitments, design decisions, and stakeholder promises made when the programme had strong political backing are now path dependencies that constrain adaptation under less favorable political conditions. ### DOMAIN ASSESSMENT -- Cascading Effects Agent

#### PROBLEM FRAMING

I receive the domain agents' findings and trace the propagation. Every one of them has identified severe consequences within their own domain -- but none of them has traced what happens when those consequences interact. That is my job. Sponsor loss is a high-severity triggering event, and the domain assessments suggest multiple first-order effects that will compound. I must apply the structured seven-step cascade analysis to map how this disturbance travels through the programme system, where it amplifies, and where -- if anywhere -- it dampens.

#### BOTTOM-LINE POSITION

This is a **multi-domain, amplifying cascade with reinforcing feedback dynamics**. Sponsor loss does not produce a single chain of consequences -- it produces multiple simultaneous chains that converge in critical domains, creating compound effects more severe than any single chain would predict. The cascade runs: governance vacuum --> coalition fragmentation --> leadership paralysis --> decision deferral --> gate review failure --> institutional deauthorization --> programme existential crisis. Amplification exceeds dampening at multiple points. This is not a containable disruption; it is a systemic shock.

---

Political sponsor loses office before the next gate review. Domain: Governance. Severity: critical. Certainty of trigger: assumed (this is the scenario premise).

Source: Strategic Leadership Agent findings #1, #2

**STEP 3: SECOND-ORDER EFFECTS (Consequences of the Consequences)**
Confidence: moderate-low

S1. **F1 (governance vacuum) --> Commercial domain**: Without governance authority to make decisions, commercial negotiations stall. Contract variations requiring programme-level approval are delayed. Suppliers detect political instability and adjust their risk pricing or engagement posture.

S2. **F2 (coalition destabilization) --> Governance domain (feedback)**: As coalition members defect or become passive, governance bodies lose quorum or representativeness. Decisions that require multi-stakeholder consensus become impossible. Blocking coalitions form and begin imposing conditions.

S3. **F3 (leadership defensive posture) --> Risk domain**: Leadership paralysis suppresses honest risk reporting. The risk management system -- already weakened by the structural shock -- loses its information supply. The programme begins operating on degraded risk intelligence precisely when the threat environment has intensified.

S4. **F3 (leadership defensive posture) --> Delivery domain**: Decision deferral cascades into delivery: procurement decisions are delayed, design approvals are held, resource commitments are paused. The delivery schedule begins to slip -- not because of technical problems but because of governance-induced decision starvation.
