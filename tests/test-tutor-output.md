CONCEPT: Escalation of Commitment

DEFINITION
Escalation of commitment is the pattern whereby decision-makers continue investing resources — money, time, political capital, institutional credibility — in a failing course of action, despite accumulating evidence that the endeavour is unlikely to succeed. It is not a single bad decision but a sequence of decisions, each individually defensible in isolation ("we've come this far"), that collectively produce an outcome no rational actor would have chosen at the outset.

In MMPM doctrine, escalation of commitment is understood as the most dangerous programme pathology. It is the mechanism through which major programmes that should be stopped, restructured, or fundamentally re-scoped instead absorb ever-greater resources on a trajectory toward catastrophic failure. The pathology is driven by the convergence of psychological forces (sunk cost attachment, self-justification, loss aversion), social forces (groupthink, reputational lock-in, blame avoidance), organisational forces (institutional inertia, reporting distortion, absence of stopping rules), and political forces (public commitment by senior leaders, electoral or ministerial exposure, coalition maintenance).

Critically, escalation is not about irrational individuals. It is a systemic phenomenon. The programme's governance architecture, incentive structure, information flows, decision culture, and political context create conditions that make continued commitment the path of least resistance — even when discontinuation would be the rational choice. A programme director trapped in an escalation dynamic is not stupid; they are operating within a system that has made stopping harder than continuing.

DOCTRINAL CONTEXT
Primary: Module 4 — Major Programme Risk. Escalation of commitment is a core concept in the MMPM risk curriculum, grounded in Drummond's research on megaproject escalation dynamics. Drummond's work demonstrates how psychological, social, and organisational forces interact to trap major programmes in escalation spirals, with detailed case analysis of programmes that continued absorbing resources well past rational stopping points. The concept connects directly to Kahneman's inside view / outside view distinction and the planning fallacy: escalating programmes are characterised by persistent reliance on the inside view ("our revised plan will work this time") in the face of outside-view evidence that comparable programmes in similar positions did not recover.

Flyvbjerg's empirical research on the "iron law of megaproject management" — over budget, over time, under benefits, over and over again — provides the statistical context. Escalation of commitment is one of the primary mechanisms through which the iron law perpetuates: programmes that encounter cost and schedule overruns respond not by re-evaluating viability but by requesting additional funding and revised timelines, each time accompanied by a refreshed optimistic forecast.

Secondary: Module 6 — Strategic Leadership and Programme Performance. The Strategic Leadership curriculum addresses escalation from the decision-culture and leadership-behaviour perspective. Kahneman, Lovallo, and Sibony's work on decision quality provides the cognitive framing: escalation is a decision quality failure driven by identifiable biases (sunk cost fallacy, confirmation bias, overconfidence, anchoring to the original plan). Roberto's research, including the Everest case, demonstrates how cognitive bias, psychological safety failures, and system complexity interact to produce catastrophic outcomes when leaders cannot recognise or act on disconfirming evidence.

The concept also connects to Module 6's treatment of incentive architecture: incentive structures that punish programme cancellation (career damage, political embarrassment, contractual penalties) and reward continuation (continued funding, continued employment, continued political narrative) create structural conditions for escalation regardless of individual rationality.

Supporting: Module 2 — Systems Thinking. Systems dynamics provides a structural explanation for escalation through reinforcing feedback loops. A cost overrun triggers a request for additional funding, which triggers increased political commitment ("we have invested too much to stop now"), which makes future stopping even harder, which enables further overruns. Causal loop analysis reveals escalation as a reinforcing loop with weak or absent balancing mechanisms — the governance interventions that should trigger re-evaluation are either absent, too slow, or politically neutralised.

Key authors: Drummond (escalation dynamics in megaprojects), Kahneman (planning fallacy, inside/outside view, sunk cost psychology), Lovallo and Kahneman (delusions of success), Flyvbjerg (iron law, reference class forecasting, strategic misrepresentation), Roberto (decision failures under complexity), Staw (original escalation of commitment research in organisational behaviour).

WHY IT MATTERS FOR PROGRAMME MANAGEMENT
Escalation of commitment is not an academic curiosity — it is the single most reliable predictor of catastrophic programme failure. Programme directors must care about it for five reasons:

1. It is the default trajectory. The structural conditions for escalation — sunk costs, public commitment, political exposure, institutional inertia, absence of stopping rules — are present in virtually every major programme. Escalation is the path of least resistance; non-escalation requires active, designed intervention.

2. It compounds every other risk. A programme suffering from technical failure, commercial dispute, or stakeholder opposition has a chance of recovery if decision-makers can recognise the situation and adapt. But when escalation dynamics are operating, disconfirming evidence is rationalised away, alternative courses of action are suppressed, and the programme continues on a trajectory that makes recovery progressively less possible. Escalation turns recoverable problems into terminal ones.

3. It is invisible from inside the programme. The inside view — "we have a revised plan, we have learned from the problems, the next phase will be different" — is indistinguishable from genuine adaptive leadership and from escalation denial. Only the outside view (reference class data, independent review, structured stopping-rule assessment) can distinguish between a programme that is genuinely adapting and one that is escalating. Programme directors who rely solely on internal signals will not detect their own escalation.

4. It corrupts governance. As escalation deepens, the programme's reporting systems bend to support continuation. Performance narratives become advocacy documents. Risk registers omit the most uncomfortable risks. Board papers frame continuation as the only viable option. Governance bodies that should be challenging the programme instead become complicit in its continuation because they, too, are invested. The governance system that was designed to prevent exactly this outcome becomes part of the escalation mechanism.

5. It destroys public value. Every resource consumed by an escalating programme is a resource unavailable for programmes that could deliver genuine public benefit. The opportunity cost of escalation — measured in deferred investments, foregone alternatives, and eroded institutional credibility — often exceeds the direct cost of the failing programme itself.

The practical implication: programme governance must be designed with explicit anti-escalation architecture. This means pre-committed stopping rules (defined at approval, before sunk costs accumulate), mandatory independent review at continuation decision points, reference class checks at each stage gate, structured pre-mortem analysis before major re-baseline decisions, and governance arrangements that make stopping a legitimate and non-punitive outcome.

RELATED CONCEPTS
- Sunk cost fallacy — the psychological mechanism underpinning escalation at the individual level; the tendency to continue an endeavour because of previously invested resources rather than future expected returns
- Reference class forecasting (Flyvbjerg) — the primary outside-view discipline against escalation; forces comparison with base rates from comparable programmes rather than reliance on the programme's own revised forecasts
- Inside view vs. outside view (Kahneman) — the cognitive framework that explains why escalation is invisible from within; programme teams reasoning from their specific plan cannot see the distributional evidence that comparable programmes in their position did not recover
- Optimism bias and strategic misrepresentation — the twin forces that produce unrealistic initial estimates, setting the stage for subsequent overruns and escalation
- Iron law of megaproject management (Flyvbjerg) — the empirical pattern that escalation helps perpetuate
- Groupthink — the social dynamic that suppresses dissent within programme boards, removing the internal challenge that might interrupt escalation
- Decision quality vs. outcome quality (Kahneman, Lovallo, Sibony) — the framework for evaluating whether continuation decisions are genuinely well-reasoned or merely path-dependent
- Decisiveness theatre — the leadership pathology where leaders reaffirm commitment to a failing programme as a display of strength, conflating persistence with good leadership
- Goodhart's Law / Campbell's Law — the performance measurement distortions that enable escalation by constructing a reported programme reality that differs from actual programme health
- Pre-mortem analysis — a structural debiasing technique that asks "assume this programme has failed; explain why" before major commitment decisions, surfacing escalation risks before they lock in

COMMON MISUNDERSTANDINGS
1. "Escalation is about stubborn individuals." This is the most pervasive misconception. Escalation is a systemic phenomenon, not a character defect. Replacing the programme director does not cure escalation if the governance structure, incentive architecture, political context, and information environment remain unchanged. Drummond's research is explicit: the forces driving escalation are structural and institutional, not personal.

2. "Escalation means the programme should always be stopped." Not necessarily. The question is not whether to stop but whether the decision to continue is being made on rational grounds — with honest appraisal of remaining costs, realistic assessment of achievable benefits, genuine comparison with alternatives (including cancellation), and reference class evidence. Some programmes that appear to be escalating are in fact making defensible continuation decisions. The test is whether the decision process is sound, not whether the programme has experienced overruns.

3. "Good risk management prevents escalation." Standard risk management — risk registers, RAG statuses, mitigation plans — is largely ineffective against escalation because escalation operates through the governance and decision-making system that risk management reports into. If the board receiving the risk report is itself subject to escalation dynamics, the risk report will be rationalised, reframed, or ignored. Anti-escalation requires structural interventions above the level of the programme's own risk management: independent review, external reference class checks, pre-committed stopping rules, and governance arrangements that protect the legitimacy of discontinuation decisions.

4. "Escalation happens suddenly." It does not. Escalation is gradual, incremental, and self-reinforcing. Each individual continuation decision appears marginal ("we only need 10% more funding to reach the next milestone"). The cumulative effect is visible only from the outside view, looking at the full trajectory of commitments against the original baseline. This incrementalism is what makes escalation so dangerous — there is rarely a single decision point where the escalation is obvious.

5. "Sunk cost reasoning is always irrational." In programme contexts, sunk costs can create real switching costs — contractual obligations, decommissioning expenses, political consequences of cancellation, reputational damage — that are forward-looking, not backward-looking. The analytical challenge is distinguishing between genuine forward-looking switching costs (rational to consider) and psychological sunk cost attachment (irrational). Escalation of commitment occurs when the latter masquerades as the former.
