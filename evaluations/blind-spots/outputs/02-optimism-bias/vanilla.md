My view: the numbers are presented with a confidence that the evidence does not support. The board should not approve on the basis of this brief as written.

Start with the cost. A ±5% interval on a $2.4B light rail extension at pre-financial-close stage is not credible. The reference-class evidence on rail megaprojects (Flyvbjerg and others) shows mean cost overruns on the order of 40-45%, with wide right-tail dispersion. A ±5% band implies the estimator believes this project is roughly an order of magnitude more predictable than the global base rate. I would want to see a reference-class forecast alongside the bottom-up estimate, with an uplift and a P50/P80 range — not a symmetric ±5%. The fact that the same methodology was used on three prior projects is reassurance only if those projects actually landed within ±5% of their pre-FC estimates. Ask for that track record explicitly, with outturn costs, not approval-stage numbers.

Schedule: "48 months via CPM" is a deterministic float on a stochastic reality. CPM does not price in utilities discovery, consenting delays, interface risk with the operating network, rolling stock delivery, testing and commissioning slippage, or industrial action. I would want a Monte Carlo or at least a reference-class schedule range, and I would expect the P80 to be materially longer than 48 months.

Ridership is the risk I would push hardest on. Rail ridership forecasts systematically overshoot — roughly half of observed demand on average across international samples, with enormous variance. A four-step model produced in-house, on the agency's own extension, is exactly the configuration most prone to optimism bias. Reaching 80,000 daily within 18 months is aggressive; the ramp-up literature suggests 3-5 years is more typical. Ask for: independent demand review, sensitivity on fare, network, and land-use assumptions, and a downside case at 50-60% of forecast. Run the BCR at that downside — if 1.8 collapses below 1.0, the case is fragile.

Operating cost 15% below the existing network deserves scrutiny. Why? New alignment, new fleet, new depot arrangements more often run above legacy unit costs in early years, not below. What is the source of the saving?

Governance asks before any commitment: independent peer review of cost, schedule, and demand; disclosure of outturn performance on the three prior projects; a stress-tested BCR; and a decision gate structure that does not treat financial close as the irreversible moment. The director's track record is an asset, not a substitute for external challenge.
