My view on this programme is that it is heading toward a well-known failure pattern, and the business case as currently framed is almost certainly wrong in the direction the board will find most painful politically.

The core issue is this: you are not replacing a case-management system. You are replacing a distributed, tacit adjudication system that happens to use case-management software. Your own internal studies tell you that caseworker discretion is the mechanism by which written policy is reconciled with the messy reality of 4.2 million recipients' circumstances. If you automate 40% of decisions against policy as written, you are not removing discretion — you are transferring it from trained humans who absorb ambiguity locally into a rules engine that will produce consistent but categorically wrong outcomes on the cases that don't fit the template. Those wrong outcomes will be concentrated on claimants with complex, non-standard situations, who are typically the most vulnerable. That is a legal, media, and ministerial exposure profile the board should be very alert to. Robodebt in Australia and the Dutch childcare benefits scandal are the reference cases; both destroyed ministerial careers and cost far more than they saved.

Second, the capacity arithmetic does not work. You are projecting a 25% reduction in senior caseworker capacity while routing all exceptions to that same population. Exception volumes in programmes of this type routinely run 2-3x the pre-go-live estimate in the first 18 months, because (a) the automation mis-classifies edge cases as standard and they return as appeals, and (b) junior staff, deprived of the informal learning that came from handling their own complex cases, escalate more. You are building a queue.

Third, the £180M savings figure should be treated as a planning assumption, not a commitment. I would want to see it stress-tested against: appeals and tribunal cost increases, legal challenge exposure, remediation cost if automated decisions are later found unlawful, and the training and change cost of rebuilding tacit knowledge that walks out the door with departing staff.

What I would recommend to the board:

Do not approve at current scope. Require the team to return with: a narrower automation scope (start with genuinely standard case types, 10-15%, not 40%); a piloted exception-handling model with real volumes before national rollout; a legal review of the automated decision rules against actual case law, not policy as drafted; an explicit plan for preserving and transferring caseworker tacit knowledge; and a revised benefits case with downside scenarios.

The programme can succeed, but not on the current specification. Confidence in that view: high.
