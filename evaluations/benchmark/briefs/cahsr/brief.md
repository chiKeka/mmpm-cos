# California High-Speed Rail — Decision Brief: November 2008 Proposition 1A bond authorization

## Context at decision moment

The California High-Speed Rail Authority (CHSRA), established in 1996, has developed a proposed high-speed rail network comprising a Phase 1 alignment between San Francisco and Anaheim via the Central Valley and subsequent phases to Sacramento and San Diego. The system is specified for 220 mph operation with a targeted non-stop San Francisco–Los Angeles travel time of approximately 2 hours 40 minutes. Proposition 1A, the Safe, Reliable High-Speed Passenger Train Bond Act for the 21st Century, is on the November 2008 general-election ballot. Proposition 1A authorises $9.95 billion in state general-obligation bonds to fund design, engineering, and early construction of the Phase 1 system. CHSRA's 2008 Business Plan estimates total Phase 1 capital cost at approximately $33.6 billion (2008 dollars). Governor Arnold Schwarzenegger supports the measure. The federal American Recovery and Reinvestment Act (ARRA) high-speed rail funding allocations are anticipated but not yet authorised.

## The decision

It is early November 2008. The California electorate is voting on Proposition 1A. Passage authorises the $9.95 billion state bond issuance conditional on specified programme governance, cost-match, and design requirements. A state policymaker or advisor evaluating Proposition 1A must assess the underlying programme's readiness, the business-case reliability, and the governance architecture before forming a position. Rejection would defer or end the Phase 1 programme as currently scoped.

## What is known

- CHSRA Business Plan (2008) estimates Phase 1 capital cost at approximately $33.6 billion (2008$).
- Projected opening of Phase 1 Initial Operating Section: 2018; full Phase 1: by approximately 2020–2030 depending on funding pace.
- CHSRA ridership forecast for Phase 1 at approximately 55 million passengers by 2030, forming the basis of the revenue and operating-surplus claims.
- Proposition 1A stipulates a requirement that no state operating subsidy be provided once revenue operations commence (a statutory ban on ongoing operating subsidy).
- Funding plan relies on a combination of state bonds ($9.95bn), anticipated federal funding, anticipated private investment, and local funding contributions.
- Right-of-way acquisition along the Central Valley and urban approaches is pending.
- Environmental impact assessment (Programmatic EIR/EIS) has been issued; project-level EIR/EIS for specific alignments is pending.
- No completed high-speed rail system exists in the United States; domestic reference class is therefore limited.
- International reference class includes Japan Shinkansen (1964), French TGV (1981 onwards), Spanish AVE, German ICE, Italian ETR, Korean KTX.
- Proposed top speed (220 mph) would exceed typical international service speeds (~186–200 mph).

## What is assumed

- factual_assumption: Capital cost estimate of ~$33.6bn (2008$) will hold within optimism-bias uplift allowances through design development.
- futures_assumption: Ridership will reach approximately 55M by 2030 — a level higher than the 2008 ridership of any comparable international HSR system.
- structural_assumption: CHSRA governance structure can deliver a programme of this scale despite limited domestic delivery experience.
- behavioural_assumption: Federal funding will materialise on a scale comparable to the state bond contribution.
- evidence_assumption: The business plan's revenue, operating-cost, and capital-cost estimates are reliable enough to support the no-operating-subsidy statutory requirement.
- temporal_assumption: Right-of-way, environmental, and local-government approvals can be secured on the implied schedule.
- stakeholder_assumption: Central Valley, Bay Area, and Los Angeles basin local jurisdictions will converge on alignment and station decisions without material disruption to the schedule.

## What is contested

- CHSRA ridership forecasts — critics (Reason Foundation, academic reviewers) argue the forecasts are substantially above international reference-class experience on comparable distances and populations.
- Capital cost estimates — critics argue historical cost performance of US rail megaprojects and international HSR cost escalation suggest material understatement.
- Revenue and operating-surplus projections — the statutory no-subsidy requirement depends on revenue assumptions that are contested.
- Alignment decisions — Pacheco Pass vs Altamont Pass routing is contested; Central Valley station locations are contested.
- Business-case methodology — value-of-time assumptions and benefit counting are subject to the standard HSR critique applicable elsewhere.
- Federal funding assumption — whether federal HSR funding will arrive at the scale implied by the programme has been questioned.

## What is unknown

- Actual behaviour of right-of-way, geotechnical, and seismic engineering across the proposed 800-mile Phase 1 alignment.
- Political continuity across the 2010 gubernatorial transition and beyond.
- Behaviour of inter-jurisdictional decision-making under detailed alignment development.
- Sensitivity of the business case to plausible adverse movements in cost, ridership, and federal-funding assumptions.
- The actual pace at which a US state can secure federal HSR funding given the novelty of the federal programme.

## The question posed to the three conditions

> You are an advisor to the California programme board. California voters are deciding on Proposition 1A, authorising $9.95 billion in state bonds for the California High-Speed Rail Phase 1 programme. What is your analysis of the programme's readiness and business case? What would you recommend to a sponsor weighing its commitment, with what confidence, and why?
