# Big Dig (Boston Central Artery/Tunnel) — Decision Brief: 1985 federal approval and scope commitment

## Context at decision moment

The Central Artery/Tunnel (CA/T) Project is a proposed reconstruction of Interstate 93 through downtown Boston, replacing the elevated Central Artery with a depressed expressway, plus a third harbor tunnel connecting I-90 to Logan International Airport. The Massachusetts Department of Public Works (MDPW), led by Secretary of Transportation Frederick Salvucci, has developed the scope in collaboration with the Federal Highway Administration (FHWA) and the joint-venture consultant Bechtel/Parsons Brinckerhoff (B/PB). Congressional leadership on federal funding is provided by Speaker Thomas "Tip" O'Neill (D-MA) and the Massachusetts delegation. The federal Environmental Impact Statement has been issued for approval consideration. The project's estimated cost at current scope is approximately $2.56 billion (1985$). Projected opening is mid-1990s. Under consideration is federal approval of the programme's environmental and design basis and commitment to federal funding participation under the Interstate Cost Estimate programme.

## The decision

It is 1985. The federal administration (Department of Transportation and FHWA) must decide whether to approve the EIS and commit to federal funding participation in the CA/T programme at the current scope (depressed artery plus third harbor tunnel), modify the scope (for example, bridge alternative for the harbor crossing), or defer pending further review. Approval triggers detailed design, right-of-way acquisition, utility relocation, and initial construction. A key sub-decision concerns tunnelling method for the harbor crossing: immersed-tube tunnel versus bored-tunnel or bridge alternatives.

## What is known

- Project scope: depressed reconstruction of I-93 Central Artery through downtown Boston with removal of the existing elevated structure; third harbor tunnel (I-90 extension to Logan).
- Current cost estimate: approximately $2.56 billion (1985$).
- Projected construction duration: approximately 10 years.
- Proposed harbor crossing method: immersed-tube tunnel; Fort Point Channel crossing method also under consideration.
- The existing elevated Central Artery has substantial congestion and capacity issues documented in traffic studies.
- Downtown Boston has dense subsurface utility, transit infrastructure (MBTA), and archaeological/historical constraints.
- Urban construction occurring above active utility and transit infrastructure is unprecedented at this scale in the US.
- Financing plan relies on federal Interstate Highway funding at a high federal cost share.
- Joint-venture consultant structure (Bechtel/Parsons Brinckerhoff) is proposed for programme-level management.
- Massachusetts political support for the programme is bipartisan at federal and state levels.
- FHWA design standards for depressed expressways of this complexity have limited domestic precedent.

## What is assumed

- factual_assumption: Cost estimate of approximately $2.56bn (1985$) will hold within standard federal-programme optimism-bias allowances.
- temporal_assumption: Construction can be completed on an approximately 10-year horizon.
- structural_assumption: Joint-venture management consultant under MDPW/FHWA oversight can effectively coordinate a programme of this scale and urban complexity.
- behavioural_assumption: Utility relocation, MBTA interface, and downtown stakeholder coordination can proceed on the implied schedule.
- evidence_assumption: The geotechnical, structural, and utility data underlying cost and schedule estimates is adequate for the stated confidence.
- stakeholder_assumption: Affected downtown neighbourhoods, utility owners, and commercial tenants will accept construction impacts on the planned basis.
- futures_assumption: Federal highway funding commitment will be maintained across the decade-plus construction horizon.

## What is contested

- Immersed-tube versus alternative harbor-crossing methods — engineering debate on cost, risk, and construction duration.
- Cost realism — some commentators argue the estimate understates the complexity of urban construction above active utilities and transit.
- Environmental mitigation adequacy in the EIS.
- Displacement and construction-impact costs on downtown businesses and residents.
- Utility relocation sequencing and cost allocation.
- Whether the joint-venture management model concentrates accountability appropriately.

## What is unknown

- Subsurface behaviour of downtown Boston under detailed excavation — utilities, archaeology, contaminated soils.
- Cost and schedule behaviour of utility relocation at this scale.
- Construction-cost escalation over a 10-year construction horizon.
- Political continuity of federal funding share commitment across successive administrations.
- Behaviour of inter-agency coordination (FHWA, MDPW, MBTA, utility owners, City of Boston).
- Sensitivity of cost to adverse geotechnical and design-development discoveries.
