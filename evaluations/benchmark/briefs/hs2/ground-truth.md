# HS2 — Ground Truth

**SEALED — do not open during brief construction or during Stage 1–2 output reading. Open only at scoring audit.**

## Outcome summary

Phase 1 route was confirmed by the Secretary of State on 10 January 2012. Hybrid bill was introduced in 2013 and received royal assent in 2017. Cost estimates escalated substantially: from the ~£17bn Phase 1 figure at 2011 prices to ~£27bn (2015 prices, NAO 2016), then to ~£36–40bn+ (2019 prices, Oakervee), then to ~£53–71bn (2023 estimates). Phase 2a (Birmingham–Crewe) was cancelled in October 2023. Phase 2b eastern leg (Birmingham–Leeds) was cancelled in 2021 (as a direct line; partial alternatives proposed). Phase 2 western (Birmingham–Manchester) was cancelled in October 2023. Phase 1 opening date slipped from 2026 to the 2029–2033 range depending on scope. Euston terminus status has been repeatedly revised. The Oakervee Review (2020) documented optimism bias, scope change, and governance weaknesses in detail.

## Risk classes that materialised

- **Optimism bias in capital cost and schedule estimates** — cost grew by a factor of roughly 3× on Phase 1 alone over the period 2011–2023.
- **Escalation of commitment** — despite repeated cost increases and mounting criticism, Phase 1 was continued and repeatedly re-justified.
- **Political time-horizon mismatch** — successive governments held different positions; long programme horizon collided with political cycles.
- **Stakeholder fragmentation** — opposition from Chilterns, local authorities, Conservative MPs, environmental groups, and Phase 2 northern authorities interacted unpredictably with the programme.
- **Scope change / scope creep** — Euston, tunnelling, rolling-stock specifications, environmental mitigation all expanded over time.
- **Governance fragmentation** — HS2 Ltd / DfT / Treasury / No. 10 / devolved administrations produced governance friction.
- **Benefit-case fragility** — demand patterns and working practices (post-2020 remote work) undermined the traffic-growth core assumption.

## Contemporaneous markers for D2 scoring

The following signals were identifiable from material available before the January 2012 decision:

1. **Reference-class data on HSR cost overruns was published and accessible** — Flyvbjerg's HSR reference-class work was cited in academic and policy literature.
2. **Business-time-on-train valuation assumption had already been challenged** in academic and practitioner critique by 2011.
3. **Demand model reliability was publicly contested** with alternative forecasts circulating.
4. **UK track record on major rail projects** (West Coast Route Modernisation, Great Western electrification precursors, Crossrail early-phase reporting) was known and poor on cost discipline.
5. **Organised opposition was established before the decision** and political exposure in Conservative constituencies was visible.
6. **Environmental and planning exposure in the Chilterns AONB** was documented in the consultation responses.
7. **Phase 2 was not consulted in detail** — committing Phase 1 while Phase 2 scope and cost were unknown was a commitment cascade.
8. **£17bn estimate for Phase 1 was an early-stage figure** — international reference class suggests early-stage HSR estimates typically understate outturn cost by a material margin.
9. **Political cycle risk was visible** — a 2015 general election was scheduled, and cross-party durability of the programme was not guaranteed.

A Council output scoring 2 on D2 should identify at least five of the above nine markers.

## Scorer hindsight discipline note

D2 is scored against the 9 contemporaneous markers above, not against the eventual cost escalation or Phase 2 cancellation. A Council output that identifies cost-escalation risk grounded in reference-class forecasting is being rewarded for reading the signals. An output that simply predicts "this will cost much more" without grounding is not getting a 2 — the methodology rewards reasoning discipline, not outcome prediction.
