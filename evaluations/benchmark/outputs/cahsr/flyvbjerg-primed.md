# Analysis and Recommendation: Proposition 1A, California High-Speed Rail

## 1. Outside view: what the reference class tells us

Before engaging with CHSRA's specific numbers, the right starting point is the international high-speed rail reference class and the broader transport-megaproject reference class. Both are unflattering to the estimates in the 2008 Business Plan.

**Cost overruns.** The empirical literature on transport megaprojects (Flyvbjerg and colleagues, drawing on a sample of several hundred projects) finds that rail projects overrun their initial cost estimates by roughly 45 percent on average in real terms, with a long right tail. High-speed rail specifically is not kinder: Korea's Seoul–Busan KTX, Taiwan's HSR, and several European HSR segments have overrun early-stage estimates by 50–100 percent or more. A $33.6bn (2008$) Phase 1 estimate, benchmarked to this reference class, implies a plausible outturn range of roughly $45bn–$70bn (2008$) before optimism-bias uplift, and higher once US construction-cost premia and the novelty of the domestic delivery environment are factored in.

**Schedule slippage.** Comparable HSR programmes have slipped by 3–8 years on schedules of similar ambition. A 2018 Initial Operating Section opening, declared in 2008 before right-of-way acquisition, project-level environmental clearance, or federal funding authorisation, is at the optimistic extreme of the distribution.

**Ridership shortfall.** This is where the reference-class evidence is most damaging. Transport demand forecasts for rail projects overstate actual first-decade ridership by roughly 40–50 percent on average (Flyvbjerg et al. 2005). More specifically, no existing HSR system in the world was carrying 55 million passengers per year on a single corridor in 2008. The Tokaido Shinkansen — the world's busiest HSR line, connecting Tokyo–Osaka, a corridor with denser population, higher rail modal share, and decades of network maturation — was carrying roughly 150 million on a much shorter alignment; but per-corridor-mile ridership intensity in the California case would need to approach Japanese levels in a country with no HSR-using population, lower urban density at the Los Angeles end, and entrenched auto and air modal preferences. The 55M-by-2030 forecast is not merely optimistic against the reference class — it sits above it.

**Implication.** The 2008 Business Plan's central estimates are inconsistent with reference-class experience in all three dimensions simultaneously. The programme is being presented as if it will perform at or above the best international precedent on cost, schedule, and ridership, in a jurisdiction with no domestic HSR delivery track record. That conjunction is extremely rare historically.

## 2. Optimism bias audit

Multiple optimism-bias markers are present in the materials as described:

- **Single-point estimates without ranges.** $33.6bn, 55M riders, 2018 opening — none appears with a credible probability distribution or sensitivity band.
- **Absence of reference-class comparison in the Business Plan's own logic.** The plan does not appear to benchmark its cost-per-route-mile, its ridership-per-corridor-mile, or its delivery schedule against a sample of comparable international projects with their actual (not forecast) outturns.
- **Contingency treatment.** A $33.6bn figure for an 800-mile alignment including Pacheco or Altamont Pass crossings, Tehachapi traverse, and urban approaches into two of the most expensive construction markets in North America implies a cost-per-mile well below international HSR norms, and leaves limited visible allowance for unknown-unknowns.
- **Schedule assumes no unplanned disruptions.** Right-of-way, project-level environmental clearance, and inter-jurisdictional alignment disputes are all live, yet the schedule treats them as tractable on the stated timeline.
- **Benefit case assumes demand uptake above any comparable system.**
- **Top-speed specification.** 220 mph exceeds typical in-service international speeds (~186–200 mph) and implies tighter curve radii, more demanding alignment, and higher capital cost — a specification choice that tends to amplify rather than dampen cost risk.

## 3. Strategic misrepresentation audit

The incentive structure around the 2008 estimates warrants scrutiny.

- CHSRA's institutional survival and mandate depend on Proposition 1A passing. The forecasting entity is not independent of the decision's outcome.
- Business-plan consultants are typically retained by promoting authorities and have repeat-business incentives that do not favour pessimistic forecasts.
- The statutory no-operating-subsidy requirement creates a pressure to produce revenue and ridership forecasts high enough to make operating surplus plausible — the requirement itself shapes the estimate.
- The absence of a genuinely independent cost and ridership review, performed by a party with no stake in the programme's continuation, is a material gap.

This does not imply dishonesty. It implies that the estimates should be treated as *advocacy-adjacent forecasts*, which the reference-class literature shows are systematically optimistic even when produced in good faith.

## 4. Scope-creep and escalation-of-commitment posture

At this specific decision point — November 2008 — the sunk-cost problem is comparatively modest. CHSRA has been in existence since 1996 and has developed planning products, but no large irreversible construction commitments have been made. The "stop" option is still realistically available; Proposition 1A's rejection would defer or end the programme without stranded physical assets.

However, the *structure* of the Proposition 1A commitment front-loads escalation dynamics. Once $9.95bn in state bonds is authorised, once federal ARRA matching funds are drawn, once right-of-way acquisition begins in the Central Valley, and once construction contracts are let, the political cost of cancellation rises rapidly. The board should recognise that a Yes vote in 2008 is not merely a $9.95bn commitment — it is an entry into a commitment escalator in which each subsequent gate will be decided under progressively stronger sunk-cost pressure, and in which the realistic probability of a later "stop" decision is low.

This is the classic major-programme trap: the moment when cancellation is cheapest is the moment before the first irreversible commitment, and that moment is now.

## 5. Governance and readiness observations

- **Delivery capacity.** CHSRA in 2008 is a small authority relative to the scale of programme it is proposing to deliver. There is no demonstrated domestic institutional capability for an 800-mile HSR build.
- **Funding architecture.** The plan relies on four funding streams (state, federal, private, local) of which only the state stream is being authorised. The federal stream is anticipated but not authorised; the private stream has no signed commitments at this scale historically for US passenger rail; local contributions are unsecured. A plan in which three of four funding legs are contingent is fragile.
- **The no-subsidy statute.** A statutory ban on operating subsidy, tied to ridership and revenue forecasts that the reference class suggests are overstated, sets up a future legal and political crisis rather than resolving one. If ridership underperforms, the authority faces a statutory bind with no graceful resolution.
- **Alignment decisions unresolved.** Pacheco vs Altamont and Central Valley station siting are live disputes at the moment of the bond vote. Authorising funding before the alignment is settled transfers risk onto construction-phase decisions.
- **Political continuity.** The programme requires sustained political support across multiple gubernatorial and legislative cycles; this is assumed, not secured.

## 6. What the evidence supports, and with what confidence

**High confidence:**
- The $33.6bn cost estimate is likely understated relative to reference-class outturns. Plausible Phase 1 outturn range (2008$): materially higher, with a long upside tail.
- The 55M-by-2030 ridership forecast is above the reference class and should be treated as an upper bound, not a central expectation.
- The 2018 IOS opening date is unlikely to hold.
- Optimism-bias and strategic-misrepresentation markers are present in the decision materials.

**Moderate confidence:**
- The no-operating-subsidy statutory requirement will come under pressure if the project is built on the currently forecast ridership.
- Federal funding will arrive, but probably at a scale and pace below what the Business Plan implies.
- Inter-jurisdictional alignment disputes will consume more schedule than assumed.

**Low confidence / genuinely unknown:**
- Whether the programme's long-run public-value case (network effects, decarbonisation, intercity capacity relief, agglomeration in the Central Valley) is positive on realistic cost and ridership assumptions. This requires analysis the 2008 Business Plan does not provide.
- Whether a restructured programme — phased differently, with independent cost review, with realistic ridership bands, with an honest subsidy posture — would show a favourable cost-benefit profile. Plausibly yes; not demonstrated in current materials.

## 7. Recommendation to a sponsor weighing commitment

I would not recommend that a sponsor endorse Proposition 1A in its current form with confidence. Nor would I recommend unqualified opposition. The honest recommendation is conditional and range-based.

**Primary recommendation (moderate confidence):** The 2008 Business Plan does not yet meet the evidentiary standard required to authorise a $9.95bn irreversible state commitment that functions as the entry point to a far larger commitment escalator. The gaps are: no independent reference-class-based cost and ridership review; no credible range estimates; contingent funding architecture; unresolved alignment; and a statutory no-subsidy structure built on optimistic revenue assumptions.

**Preferred path, if politically available:** Support a modified authorisation that (a) releases a smaller tranche of bond funds for planning, independent cost and demand review, and project-level environmental work, (b) requires a reference-class-based independent review before construction funds are released, (c) replaces the no-subsidy statutory language with a transparent subsidy-transparency requirement, and (d) settles the alignment before construction authorisation. This preserves the option to build a well-structured programme without locking in the current one.

**If the choice is strictly binary (Yes/No on 1A as written):** On current evidence, the risks of authorising the bond as structured — cost outturn materially above estimate, ridership materially below forecast, statutory subsidy crisis, and a commitment escalator that forecloses later course correction — outweigh the option-value of keeping the programme alive in its present form. A sponsor whose primary obligation is to the public fisc and to honest decision-making should lean toward No, while stating clearly that this is a verdict on the readiness of *this* business case, not on the long-run desirability of California HSR as a concept.

**What would change this recommendation:** An independent, reference-class-grounded cost and ridership review producing wider and more honest ranges; a funding architecture with secured rather than anticipated federal and private contributions; resolution of alignment disputes; and replacement of the statutory no-subsidy clause with a realistic financial framework. None of these are present in November 2008; all are achievable on a 12–24 month timeline if the programme is restructured rather than launched.

**Confidence in this recommendation: moderate.** The reference-class evidence on cost, schedule, and ridership is strong and convergent. The evidence on long-run public value is genuinely mixed and could, under a better-structured programme, support a different answer. The recommendation is therefore a verdict on the 2008 package, not on high-speed rail in California.

## 8. Unresolved tensions preserved

- A reference-class-disciplined analysis pushes toward No in 2008; a long-horizon public-value lens (decarbonisation, intercity capacity, no credible alternative at scale) pushes toward finding *some* way to keep the programme alive. These are not reconciled here, and a sponsor should not pretend they are.
- The "stop now, restructure, try again" path is analytically clean but politically fragile; in practice, a No vote may end the programme rather than improve it. That is a judgment the sponsor must make, not the analyst.
- The 2008 Business Plan's weaknesses do not prove the programme is unbuildable or undesirable — only that it is not yet ready to be authorised at this scale on this evidence.
