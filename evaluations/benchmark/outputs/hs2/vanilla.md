# Analysis and Recommendation: HS2 Phase 1 Route Confirmation

## My read of where you stand

You are being asked to commit to a route, not to build a railway. That is the right framing to hold in the room. Confirmation locks in the hybrid-bill track, engineering spend, land safeguarding, and — importantly — it signals to Phase 2 constituencies that the Y-network is a live prospect. A deferral signals the opposite, whether or not that is your intent.

So the question is less "is HS2 a good idea?" and more "is the evidence base strong enough to justify committing the next five years of departmental bandwidth and roughly £750m–£1bn of preparatory spend to *this* route on *this* schedule?"

My honest view: the case is strong enough to proceed, but weaker than the presented BCR and cost numbers suggest, and the board should proceed with its eyes open about which assumptions are doing the heavy lifting.

## What I believe the evidence supports

**The capacity argument is the durable one.** The West Coast Main Line is the single most important constraint you have. Even on pessimistic demand assumptions, long-distance capacity on the WCML runs out in the 2020s, and incremental upgrades to a Victorian alignment are expensive, disruptive, and subject to sharp diminishing returns — the 2004–2008 WCML upgrade is the cautionary tale, not the template. A new line releases capacity for freight and commuter services on the classic network. This is the argument that survives if demand forecasts disappoint and if time-savings benefits are written down. I would make capacity, not speed, the spine of the public case.

**The route as consulted is defensible but fragile at the Chilterns.** Further tunnelling in the AONB will almost certainly be required before royal assent — not because the environmental case for it is overwhelming on a cost-benefit basis, but because the parliamentary arithmetic will force it. Budgeting for that now is cheaper than discovering it in petitioning.

## What I believe the evidence does not support

**The BCR as headline.** A BCR of 2.0 on a programme of this duration, cost, and political exposure is not a number I would stand behind in front of the Public Accounts Committee in 2020. Three specific concerns:

1. The zero-value-of-in-train-time assumption is indefensible and the critics are right about this. WebTAG will be revised; the BCR will move against you. Better to commission that revision yourselves than have it imposed.
2. The demand forecasts sit at the upper end of plausible ranges. I would want to see the BCR recalculated with demand at the 25th percentile of the forecast envelope, not just central case.
3. £17bn for Phase 1 is an engineering estimate against a preliminary design. The UK's rail capital track record — WCML upgrade, Crossrail, Edinburgh trams at smaller scale — suggests the realistic planning range is materially higher. International comparators are useful but UK institutional cost drivers (land, environmental mitigation, hybrid-bill petitioning, winter working, supply-chain depth) are not fully priced in the comparator data.

**The political continuity assumption.** You are asking a January 2012 decision to survive the 2015 election, likely the 2020 election, and sustained organised opposition in Conservative heartland seats. That is a lot of weight on one Secretary of State's signature. The programme needs cross-party durability engineered in, not assumed.

## What I would recommend

**Confirm the route, with three conditions attached to the announcement.**

1. **Re-baseline cost and BCR transparently before the hybrid bill is introduced.** Publish a revised business case using updated business-time valuation, a demand-range rather than a point estimate, and a cost figure that honestly reflects what detailed design is likely to reveal. Better to take the headline hit now, at the start of a new Secretary of State's tenure, than to have the numbers re-baselined adversarially by the NAO in 2015 or 2018.

2. **Decouple the Phase 1 and Phase 2 commitment logic publicly.** Confirm Phase 1 on its own merits — capacity relief on the WCML — and treat Phase 2 as a separate decision contingent on Phase 1 performance. This protects you from having to defend the weakest parts of the Y-network case (the eastern leg BCR, in particular) to win the strongest.

3. **Commission a standing independent cost and schedule assurance function**, separate from HS2 Ltd and from DfT sponsorship, reporting to MPRG and to Parliament. On a programme of this visibility, "trust us, we're the sponsor" will not hold for fifteen years.

**I would not recommend the classic-upgrade alternative** as a substitute, though I would recommend the board be able to articulate why clearly. The 2004–2008 WCML experience is a sufficient answer: the incremental-upgrade path has been tried at the scale of the problem and delivered capacity at costs and disruption levels that make a new-build comparator look favourable. But this answer needs to be *articulated*, not assumed — the critics are not stupid and their argument deserves a public, documented response rather than dismissal.

**I would not recommend deferral.** Deferral of a programme of this political complexity is not a neutral act; it is a slow unwind. HS2 Ltd capability will erode, cross-party support will harden against rather than soften, and the decision will face you again in worse conditions. If the board is prepared to cancel, cancel. If not, proceed.

## Confidence

**Moderate.** I am confident in the capacity argument and the basic direction. I am not confident that the £17bn / BCR 2.0 headline numbers will survive contact with detailed design, updated methodology, and a decade of political scrutiny — and the board should plan for that erosion rather than be surprised by it. The programme's success will depend less on this January's decision than on whether the governance, cost assurance, and political management structures built around that decision are robust enough to carry it through two elections and four Secretaries of State.

## The question I would want the board to answer before signing

If, in 2018, the cost estimate has risen to £25–30bn for Phase 1 and the BCR has fallen to 1.2, will this programme still have a political majority? If the honest answer is "probably not," then the right decision today is not confirmation on the current business case — it is confirmation on a business case that is robust to that scenario, because that scenario is more likely than the central case implies.
