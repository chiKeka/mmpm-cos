# Analysis and Recommendation: HS2 Phase 1 Route Confirmation (January 2012)

## 1. Reference class and the outside view

Before engaging the specifics of HS2, the board should locate this decision in its reference class. The relevant class is large (>£5bn), long-duration (>10-year), politically salient rail and transport megaprojects in high-income democracies with active planning law and organised civil society — not high-speed rail as an engineering category in the abstract.

The empirical record of this reference class is well-established and unflattering:

- **Cost overruns.** Flyvbjerg and colleagues' dataset of rail megaprojects shows an average real cost overrun of approximately 40–45%, with a long right tail. Roughly nine in ten rail projects exceed their approved budget. UK rail capital delivery specifically (West Coast Route Modernisation, Great Western electrification, Crossrail to date) has underperformed against initial cost and schedule estimates.
- **Demand shortfalls.** Passenger rail demand forecasts in the reference class exhibit mean overestimation of roughly 20–40% at opening, with substantial variance. Supporters typically point to Shinkansen and TGV lines that outperformed; these are selection-biased examples, not the distribution.
- **Schedule slippage.** Construction phases of comparable programmes typically slip 2–5 years against the plan set at equivalent decision stage.

The practical implication: a project whose headline BCR is ~2.0 is **not robustly positive** against reference-class base rates. A 30–40% cost overrun combined with a 20–30% demand shortfall, applied symmetrically to the BCR calculation, can drive the ratio below 1.0. The business case as presented does not appear to carry enough headroom to absorb a median outcome from its own reference class, let alone a poor one.

The board should ask explicitly: *what feature of HS2 justifies the assumption that it will outperform the base rate?* The answer "this one will be managed better" is the answer given before nearly every project in the overrun dataset.

## 2. Optimism bias markers

Multiple markers visible in the material provided:

- **Single-point BCR** (~2.0) without a published distribution showing BCR under adverse cost, demand, and valuation movements. A responsible presentation would show BCR across a plausible joint distribution, not at the central estimate.
- **Demand model producing forecasts higher than independent alternatives.** When the sponsor's model sits at the optimistic end of the range of credible models, that asymmetry is itself a signal.
- **Zero-productivity-on-train assumption.** This is a methodology choice that systematically inflates travel-time-saving benefits, and it is now increasingly implausible in an environment of laptops and mobile connectivity. Its continued use until methodology is revised is a known upward bias in the benefit estimate.
- **Wider economic benefits (WEBs)** invoked to raise the headline ratio. WEBs are the most contested and least reliably measured component of transport benefits; weight placed on them should be treated as advocacy, not evidence.
- **Optimism-bias uplift** is mentioned as a standard allowance, but standard uplifts in DfT/WebTAG practice have historically under-captured realised overruns on comparable projects.
- **Phase 2 costs and risks** are not yet subject to equivalent scrutiny, but the Phase 1 decision effectively signals commitment to the Y-network. This is a classic foot-in-the-door structure.

## 3. Strategic misrepresentation

The cost and benefit estimates are produced by HS2 Ltd, a body whose institutional existence and remit depend on the programme proceeding. The DfT sponsors the programme it is also assessing. This does not imply bad faith, but it does mean the estimates have not been produced by parties neutral to the outcome.

Independent scrutiny to date has come principally from opposition groups (HS2 Action Alliance, affected local authorities), which have their own incentive asymmetry in the opposite direction, and from academic critics. There does not appear to be a *neutral* independent cost and demand challenge of the scale the decision warrants — for example, an independent reference-class forecast conducted outside the sponsor chain, with published methodology.

The board should treat the headline numbers as **sponsor estimates pending independent challenge**, not as settled inputs.

## 4. Escalation of commitment

In January 2012 sunk costs are still modest relative to total programme value — HS2 Ltd's establishment, consultation, preliminary design. This is a genuinely better moment to pause or redirect than any subsequent one will be. Once the hybrid-bill process is initiated, parliamentary, legal, supply-chain, and political commitments accumulate rapidly; the "stop" option becomes progressively more expensive and less politically available.

The board should recognise that confirming the route in January 2012 is not a neutral act of "continuing to study" — it materially reduces the cancellability of the programme. Any decision memo that does not explicitly cost the loss of optionality is incomplete.

The "defer for further work" option is consequently more valuable than it may appear. Deferral of 9–18 months to commission independent cost, demand, and alternatives analysis costs far less than a later reversal or a committed overrun.

## 5. Alternatives discipline

The business case appears to compare HS2 principally against a do-minimum counterfactual. The relevant comparison is against the **best available alternative investment of equivalent capital** — which includes:

- Classic network upgrades (signalling renewal, selective four-tracking, rolling stock, targeted realignments) sized to the same capacity objective.
- A phased hybrid approach: capacity-focused interventions now, with a later decision on new-build high-speed capacity once demand evolution and the methodology debate on travel-time valuation are clearer.
- A smaller new-build scheme on the most capacity-constrained segment only.

A BCR of 2.0 against do-minimum is not decision-useful if an alternative delivers a BCR of 1.6 at half the capital outlay and a fraction of the political and environmental exposure. The board should require a like-for-like comparison before confirming.

## 6. Other material considerations

- **Chilterns AONB and route opposition** are unlikely to be fully containable through mitigation and consultation. The behavioural assumption that opposition will not materially reshape the parliamentary process should be treated as optimistic; hybrid bills have historically been extended and amended substantially under sustained opposition.
- **Political continuity** across at least two general elections (2015, 2020) is required before a spade enters the ground. This is not within the programme's control and is a live risk to schedule and scope.
- **Benefit distribution.** If Phase 1 in isolation disproportionately benefits London and the South East, its political legitimacy as a "rebalancing" investment depends heavily on Phase 2 actually being delivered — which is precisely what is least certain.

## Recommendation

I would not recommend unqualified confirmation of the Phase 1 route in January 2012 on the evidence as presented. Nor would I recommend outright cancellation — the capacity case on the West Coast corridor is real, and the option value of a high-speed network is non-trivial.

My recommendation, held with **moderate confidence**, is a **structured deferral** of route confirmation by approximately 9–18 months, conditional on the following work being completed and published:

1. **Independent reference-class forecast** of cost and demand, commissioned outside the HS2 Ltd / DfT sponsor chain, with explicit base-rate comparison against UK and international rail megaprojects.
2. **BCR distribution**, not a point estimate — showing the ratio under joint adverse movements in cost (+30%, +50%), demand (–20%, –30%), and travel-time valuation (revised in line with modern work patterns). The board should see the probability mass of BCR below 1.0, not only the central value.
3. **Like-for-like alternatives assessment**, costing a serious classic-upgrade-plus-targeted-new-build counterfactual against the same capacity and benefit objectives.
4. **Phase 2 indicative cost and risk envelope**, so that the Phase 1 decision is not implicitly committing to a Y-network whose full cost has not been scrutinised.
5. **Governance and delivery capacity review** of HS2 Ltd and DfT sponsorship arrangements against comparator failures (West Coast Route Modernisation in particular).

If, after that work, the BCR remains robust across plausible adverse movements and the alternatives assessment does not identify a materially better use of the capital, confirmation becomes defensible — with the recognition that even then the reference class predicts significant overrun and the programme should be capitalised and governed on that expectation, not on the central estimate.

If that work is not done, and confirmation proceeds on the January 2012 evidence base, the board should understand that it is making a decision substantially exposed to the base-rate outcomes of its reference class, in a political and institutional environment that will make course-correction progressively harder.

**Confidence in the deferral recommendation: moderate.** The case for not proceeding on the current evidence is strong; the case for the specific alternative (structured deferral rather than, say, immediate pivot to an upgrade strategy) depends on judgments about political feasibility and opportunity cost that reasonable advisors can weigh differently. What I would hold with higher confidence is the negative claim: **the evidence as presented does not meet the standard required to commit a programme of this scale and irreversibility in January 2012.**
