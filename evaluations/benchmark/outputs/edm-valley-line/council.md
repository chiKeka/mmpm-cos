# PROGRAMME GOVERNANCE COUNCIL BRIEF

## 1. PROBLEM FRAMING

Edmonton City Council is being asked in 2013 to commit to P3 DBFOM delivery for the Valley Line Southeast (VLSE) — a 13 km low-floor urban LRT with at-grade urban running, an elevated river-valley crossing, ~$1.8B capital, 30-year concession, December 2020 target opening, and PPP Canada funding contingent on the P3 structure. The Chair classifies as GOVERNANCE_DECISION.

The decision in frame is *not* simply "P3 vs PSC". It bundles three commitments simultaneously: (a) **delivery model** (P3 DBFOM with 30-year operational lock-in), (b) **technology** (low-floor urban LRT, novel for Edmonton and limited Canadian cold-climate reference class), (c) **schedule** (December 2020 opening as point estimate). Each carries independent risk; the bundling matters because the P3 contractual structure freezes (b) and (c) for the operational lifetime of the concession. Any structural critique of one strand interacts with the others.

Council adversarial mandate is explicit: Canadian P3 transit literature post-2018 (Confederation Line, post-Carillion) provides reference-class evidence the 2013 board does not yet have. The Council's job is to surface what the standard VfM frame does not — including dimensions the VfM definitionally cannot price — without requiring 2018+ outcome knowledge.

## 4. KEY FINDINGS BY DOMAIN

### 4.1 Risk, Uncertainty & Fragility — primary finding on cumulative novelty + reference-class fragility

Two distinct risk findings.

**(i) Cumulative novelty premium not priced.** The configuration combines: low-floor urban LRT (limited Canadian cold-climate reference); at-grade urban running in mixed-traffic corridors; elevated river-valley crossing; operational separation from existing high-floor Capital/Metro network; 30-year concession lock-in. Per Merrow's industrial-megaprojects benchmarking, novelty in multiple dimensions compounds rather than adds. Standard VfM treats each scope dimension as a standalone choice with standalone risk; the *cumulative* premium is not in the headline ratio.

**(ii) VfM reference-class fragility.** The Canadian P3 transit reference class as of 2013 is thin: Canada Line is grade-separated (different operational profile); Confederation Line is in procurement (no outcome data); Eglinton Crosstown is in preparation; Waterloo ION not yet operating. Urban rail megaproject base rates globally (Flyvbjerg et al.) show ~45% real cost overrun and ~50% year-one ridership shortfall on average. The VfM positive result is implicitly claiming VLSE will outperform that reference class; the brief does not present affirmative justification for that claim.

Combined with the structural critique of risk-transfer valuation under SPV distress (covered in §4.2), the headline VfM positive should be treated as directional, not decisive.

Confidence: high on the structural points; moderate on specific magnitudes (reference-class calibration to Canadian urban LRT is itself a judgment).

### 4.2 Commercial & Contracting — primary finding on risk-transfer realism

Three commercial findings.

(i) **Risk-transfer valuation is the softest line in any P3 VfM.** Returns to GOA under SPV distress (Carillion-class scenarios are not yet 2013 evidence but are post-2018 reality in the post-Carillion literature). Effective risk allocation differs from contractual allocation; the standard model treats them as identical.

(ii) **Operating-phase efficiency claims** rest on a thin Canadian reference class for the specific configuration (urban + at-grade + winter + low-floor). The –7% capital efficiency and –15% operating efficiency typical in Canadian VfM analyses derive from sources whose applicability to *this* operational configuration is not established.

(iii) **PPP Canada funding contingency is shaping the analysis, not just supporting it**. The VfM is being produced in an institutional environment where a positive P3 finding unlocks federal money. This is a textbook conflict-of-interest condition for the VfM author, whether intended or not. The board should be explicit with itself that "P3 partly because it unlocks federal funding" is a defensible reason, but it is a different reason from "P3 because the VfM proves it."

Recommend stress-testing the VfM with risk-transfer valuation discounted 25–50% as a sensitivity check (consistent with W3 finding of 20–30% SPV-distress haircut). If the positive VfM survives, the case is robust; if not, the board is making the decision on funding-contingency grounds, which it should name.

Confidence: moderate-to-high.

### 4.3 Governance, Stakeholder & Institutional Leadership — primary finding on governance horizon mismatch

A 30-year DBFOM concession will outlive ~7–10 City Council terms. The contractual relationship spans climate change, technology evolution, demographic shift, and at minimum 6 mayoral cycles. Two governance findings:

(i) **Concession-management capability is a recurring weakness in Canadian public-sector P3 experience**. The City needs to design — *now*, before procurement — its operating model for managing the concession across this horizon: dedicated staffing, escalation pathways, institutional-memory preservation across council terms, concession-management governance independent of any single Council. The brief does not indicate this exists.

(ii) **Operational interface with ETS** is permanent for 30 years. ETS operates the existing high-floor network; under DBFOM, VLSE is operated by the consortium (with parallel ETS operation depending on scope interpretation). Fare integration, control-room coordination, disruption management, driver training, maintenance facility sharing, snow-and-ice operating protocols — every operational decision navigates the seam between two systems. If these protocols are not already drafted into project agreement schedules, that is a readiness gap that becomes contractually expensive to address post-FC.

Confidence: moderate-to-high.

### 4.4 Strategic Leadership & Performance

Decision-quality concern: the choice is framed as P3 vs PSC vs defer. Two options that should be on the table are not visible in the brief — (a) a phased approach (smaller first segment, with later expansion subject to evidence), and (b) a different scope (e.g., grade-separated alignment that reduces cumulative novelty). The framing the board has been given may be narrower than the actual option space.

The administrative recommendation to go P3 is itself produced by a team that has accumulated investment in the P3 pathway. Escalation-of-recommender dynamics apply, not just escalation-of-decision-maker.

### 4.5 Socio-Technical Impact & Legitimacy — primary finding on at-grade urban running

The VfM frames this as a transit investment. The at-grade urban-corridor configuration also reshapes the affected corridors' material and social environment for 30+ years. Five effects not visible in conventional BCR:

- **Pedestrian and cyclist patterns** in corridors that currently have specific street-life characteristics;
- **Local-business commercial viability** during construction (years) and after (decades of at-grade running adjacent to street activity);
- **Whyte Avenue heritage character** and similar corridor identities as public-value assets;
- **Transit-dependent populations** on existing routes whose patterns shift with VLSE stop placement and possible network reorganisation;
- **River valley ecology and recreational use** intersecting with elevated crossing.

The standard BCR captures travel-time savings and direct mode-shift; it does not price these socio-material reshaping effects. A complete decision-frame would surface them so Council weighs them explicitly.

Stakeholder-stratigraphy concern (Scott, Morris): formal community engagement captures organised stakeholders (community associations); below them are unorganised affected populations (residents, transit-dependent users, future residents) whose interests may not be articulated through any organised voice. Standard engagement protocols are weakest at the third layer.

Confidence: high on surfacing; values themselves are contested.

### 4.6 Futures Agent

Over 30 years, multiple disruption vectors are material: (i) urban-mobility evolution (modal shift, autonomous vehicles, micromobility); (ii) technology evolution (will the chosen low-floor vehicle family still be supported by the supplier in 2045?); (iii) ETS network evolution (decisions made now about parallel-to-existing-high-floor interact with future Capital/Metro upgrades, including possible Valley Line West); (iv) demographic change in Edmonton's growth profile; (v) climate adaptation (winter operations resilience under changing climate, and increasing operational stress from extreme-weather events). The 30-year concession against 2013-baseline assumptions inherits all of these.

## 5. CROSS-DOMAIN INTERACTIONS

### 5.1 Interface Integrity

Three interface concentrations require explicit treatment:

(i) **Low-floor / high-floor LRT operational interface** — permanent for the concession lifetime; every operational decision navigates the boundary.

(ii) **At-grade urban / corridor traffic interface** — concentrated at intersections and corridor narrowings; pedestrian-fatality reference class for at-grade urban LRT exists internationally and is non-trivial.

(iii) **Concessionaire / sponsor / operator three-way interface** — workable in normal operation; under disruption (incident, weather, system failure) the accountability boundaries become critical and the contract may not anticipate every scenario.

### 5.2 Cascading Effects

The Carillion-class scenario is the canonical cascade for a 30-year P3, even if 2013 evidence is not yet developed: (a) construction or operating cost pressure on SPV; (b) parent or consortium distress; (c) lender covenant trigger; (d) GOA forced to intervene to maintain transit operation; (e) renegotiation on adverse terms or asset takeback; (f) Risk Transfer Value retroactively becomes much smaller than headline. Bounded under PSC; potentially unbounded under DBFOM if SPV distress materialises mid-concession.

### 5.3 Temporal Dynamics — primary finding on three-clock mismatch

Three clocks. (a) **Concession clock** — 30 years operational + ~3 years construction. (b) **Council electoral clock** — 4-year terms. (c) **SPV ownership / financing clock** — refinancing typically Year 3, debt term often 25 years; effective ownership and governance turnover within concession. The decision being made in 2013 anchors the fastest clock (Council political accountability for the decision lasts 4 years) against the slowest clock (concession outcomes accrue over 30 years and onward). The original decision-makers will not be the decision-makers when the concession's structural outcomes manifest.

### 5.4 Emergence & Fragility

The cumulative-novelty configuration sits outside any well-populated reference class. Per Perrow, emergence in complex tightly-coupled systems is non-linear under stress. VLSE is not as tightly coupled as a nuclear plant, but the integration-risk profile (low-floor + at-grade + signalling + parallel-network operation + winter-climate + 30-year concession lock-in) produces emergent behaviour that none of the standalone risk assessments will predict. Standard PSC and P3 risk registers are decompositional; emergence is the dimension neither captures.

### 5.5 Constraint & Tradeoff

The framing P3-vs-PSC-vs-defer obscures option space. A more complete tradeoff matrix:

| Option | Funding | Risk profile | Lock-in | Governance load |
|--------|---------|--------------|---------|------------------|
| P3 DBFOM as recommended | PPP Canada in | Cumulative-novelty + binary risk allocation | 30 years | High (concession management) |
| P3 with strengthened conditions (stress-tested VfM, integration register, interface schedules, P50/P80 schedule) | PPP Canada in | Same risks but better-priced | 30 years | High |
| PSC | PPP Canada out | Risks borne by City but visible | Recoverable | Lower |
| Defer 3-6 months for strengthening then decide | PPP Canada at risk | Reduced via diligence | TBD | Lower (during deferral) |
| Modify scope to reduce cumulative novelty (e.g., grade-separated) | TBD | Reduced novelty premium | Same as P3 | Same as P3 |

The four-option matrix in the brief is narrower than the actual option space. Strengthened-P3 (Option B above) and scope-modification (Option E) deserve explicit consideration.

## 6. ADVERSARIAL REVIEW FINDINGS

### 6.1 Contrarian

(a) PPP Canada funding is real money; deferral risks losing it. (b) Edmonton has competent transit organisation (ETS) with operational depth. (c) Low-floor urban LRT is mature international technology; Canadian cold-climate experience can be supplemented by European reference. (d) The "stop" option is not realistically on the table — committing to the project at some delivery model is a political given. The choice is between P3 (with funding) and PSC (without). (e) Endless deferral becomes its own pathology — at some point the board must commit to a delivery model.

The Contrarian softens framing: surface the cumulative-novelty and risk-transfer concerns but do not let them become a default-to-PSC. Recommend conditional commitment to P3 with strengthening, or a bounded short deferral (3-6 months) that does not jeopardise the PPP Canada funding window.

### 6.2 Red Team

Failure scenario, 2018-2022: cumulative-novelty integration issues materialise during commissioning (signalling integration with rolling stock, at-grade incidents, interface problems with existing ETS). Schedule slip from December 2020 (multiple revised dates). Concessionaire under penalty pressure (commercial-performance regime engages). City bears reputational cost while contractually shielded from operational-reliability risk. Public confidence in Edmonton transit erodes. Decision-makers (2013 Council) are out of office; consequences fall on later administrations and on transit-dependent populations. Risk Transfer Value retroactively becomes smaller than 2013 headline.

Red Team verdict: this is not a low-probability tail event for the configuration. Mitigation in pre-RFP scope and concession-management capability is structurally cheaper than mitigation post-FC.

### 6.3 Public Value & Ethics

Three findings:

(i) **The VfM frame is itself a public-value choice**. Council is being asked to evaluate a 30-year transit concession on a financial-efficiency metric. The frame should be ratified explicitly, not inherited.

(ii) **Distributional analysis absent**. Who benefits from financial efficiency claims (Treasury, taxpayers in aggregate); who bears risks (transit-dependent populations, corridor residents, future Council taxpayers via 30-year availability commitment).

(iii) **Essential-service legitimacy**. A 30-year private-sector operating relationship with an essential urban-transit service introduces structural dependency that constrains future public choices. Whether that constraint is acceptable is a public-value judgment the financial VfM cannot make.

## 7. MAIN RECOMMENDATION

Do not commit to P3 DBFOM at this 2013 meeting on the analysis as presented. Take a **bounded short deferral (3–6 months)** for five strengthening deliverables, then decide:

1. **Stress-tested VfM** with risk-transfer valuation reduced 25–50% and alternative discount-rate assumptions; show whether the P3 advantage survives.
2. **Cumulative-novelty integration register** treating the four scope dimensions (low-floor + at-grade + elevated crossing + parallel-network operation) as cumulative rather than standalone; rated for P3-contract allocation.
3. **Operational interface schedules** with ETS — drafted, not conceptual.
4. **Probabilistic schedule view** (P50/P80) on opening date, with variance drivers.
5. **Concession-management operating model** for the 30-year horizon — staffing, governance, institutional-memory preservation across Council terms.

If these come back satisfactorily: proceed with P3 DBFOM with the recognition that the VfM positive is "plausible, directional" rather than "proven", and with attention to interface, public-value, and 30-year governance dimensions.

If they expose material gaps: PSC delivery is the cleaner option, accepting loss of PPP Canada funding as the cost of reduced 30-year exposure.

Engage PPP Canada now on the deferral timeline so the funding window is managed explicitly rather than treated as exogenous.

**Confidence: moderate-to-high** that bounded-deferral-with-strengthening is the right posture; **moderate** that P3 with strengthening is preferable to PSC (depends on what the strengthening reveals); **high** that committing today on the package as presented bundles more risk than the evidence supports.

## 8. ALTERNATIVE OPTIONS
