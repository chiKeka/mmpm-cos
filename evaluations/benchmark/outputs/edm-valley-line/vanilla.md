# Valley Line Southeast LRT — Advisor Analysis

## Framing the decision

The board is being asked to lock in not one choice but three bundled commitments simultaneously: (1) a delivery model (P3 DBFOM), (2) a technology (low-floor urban LRT, new to Edmonton), and (3) a fixed opening date (December 2020). Each of these has independent risk. Bundling them amplifies exposure, because the P3 concession effectively freezes the technology and schedule choices for 30 years of operating consequences. A clean-eyed view of the VfM case should not obscure that the commitment being made is much broader than the procurement route.

## How I read the VfM case

The positive VfM is driven substantially by *risk transfer valuation*. That is the softest part of any P3 business case and deserves direct interrogation:

- **Return-of-risk under distress.** A consortium transfers risk only as far as its balance sheet and contractual remedies allow. If a major dispute, insolvency, or performance failure arises mid-build or mid-operations, the City is the residual risk holder — the asset is non-substitutable public infrastructure. The VfM model almost certainly does not fully price this tail. I would ask the finance team to show the VfM result with risk-transfer valuation stress-tested downward by 25–50%. If the P3 advantage disappears under that stress, the case is thinner than it appears.
- **Operating-phase efficiency claims.** These rely on a Canadian reference class that is actually quite shallow for *urban, at-grade, winter-climate, low-floor* LRT. Canada Line is grade-separated. Confederation Line is not yet operating. Waterloo ION is not yet operating. The empirical basis for claimed efficiencies in *this* operational configuration is weak.
- **Discount rate and comparator construction.** I would want independent confirmation that the Public Sector Comparator has not been inadvertently loaded with risk premia the P3 escapes by assumption.

None of this invalidates the P3 route. It does mean the VfM result should be treated as *directional*, not as a precise number.

## The issues I would press hardest

**1. Technology-and-alignment novelty stacking.** Edmonton is introducing a new rolling-stock technology (low-floor) that does not interoperate with its existing high-floor fleet, combined with an at-grade urban alignment running in mixed traffic conditions, plus an elevated river-valley structure. Each of these is manageable individually. Together, they represent a level of first-of-kind integration risk that tends to show up late in commissioning — exactly when a P3 contract structure provides the least flexibility to adapt. I would want the technical advisors to give a candid view on cumulative integration risk, not domain-by-domain risk.

**2. Operational interface with Edmonton Transit Service.** A 30-year DBFOM concession creates a permanent seam between a privately operated line and a publicly operated network. Interface questions — fare integration, control-room coordination, service disruption management, driver training, maintenance facility sharing, snow-and-ice operating protocols — need to be resolved on paper now because renegotiating them mid-concession is expensive and slow. If these protocols are not already in drafted schedules to the project agreement, that is a readiness gap.

**3. The December 2020 date.** Comparable Canadian urban LRT P3s have not reliably hit original target dates. Holding 2020 as a firm commitment in 2013 presumes smooth procurement, smooth financial close, smooth construction through dense urban environments, and smooth commissioning of novel technology. A schedule contingency view — not just a point estimate — is essential. If the board is being shown a December 2020 opening without a probabilistic range, it is being under-informed.

**4. PPP Canada funding conditionality.** Funding being contingent on P3 structure is a real financial consideration, but the board should be explicit with itself that this conditionality is *shaping* the delivery decision, not just *supporting* it. That is a legitimate basis for choosing P3, but it should be named. "We are choosing P3 partly because it unlocks federal funding" is a defensible statement. "We are choosing P3 because VfM says so" is a less complete one.

**5. Stakeholder acceptance of at-grade running.** Urban-corridor residents and businesses accepting construction-phase disruption is one question; accepting 30+ years of at-grade LRT running adjacent to road traffic is another. Legitimacy for the alignment needs to be real, not assumed. I would want to see evidence of substantive engagement with affected neighbourhoods — not just notification.

**6. Political continuity.** A 30-year concession will outlive roughly seven council terms. The governance arrangements for managing the concession — who owns the relationship with the consortium on the City side, with what institutional memory — need to be designed now. Concession management capability is a recurring weakness in Canadian public-sector P3 experience.

## My recommendation

**Do not commit to P3 DBFOM at this meeting. Take a short, structured deferral — on the order of three to six months — conditional on five specific deliverables, then decide.**

The five deliverables I would require:

1. **Stress-tested VfM.** Re-run with risk-transfer valuation reduced by 25% and 50% and with alternative discount-rate assumptions. Show the P3 advantage under each.
2. **Integration risk register.** A single consolidated register covering technology novelty (low-floor fleet), alignment novelty (at-grade urban + elevated crossing), and interface risk with the existing ETS high-floor network — rated for probability, impact, and P3-contract allocation.
3. **Operational interface schedules.** Draft schedules to the project agreement covering ETS interface, fare integration, disruption management, and maintenance coordination — not conceptual, but drafted.
4. **Probabilistic schedule view.** A P50/P80 range on opening date rather than a point estimate of December 2020, with the drivers of schedule variance identified.
5. **Concession-management design.** An explicit City-side operating model for managing a 30-year concession: staffing, reporting, escalation pathways, and how institutional knowledge is preserved across council terms.

If these five deliverables come back satisfactorily, I would support P3 DBFOM, with the caveat that the board should treat the VfM positive result as "plausible, directional" rather than "proven." If they come back with material gaps, the board should seriously consider conventional delivery, accepting the loss of PPP Canada funding as the price of reduced long-horizon exposure.

I would not recommend pursuing conventional delivery outright today. The federal funding contribution is real money, the P3 market is live, and there is a coherent case for risk transfer if the above questions can be answered. But committing today, on the package as currently presented, asks the board to accept more bundled risk than the evidence strictly supports.

## Confidence

**Moderate.** The structural concerns I have raised (novelty stacking, VfM softness on risk transfer, interface design, schedule optimism) are robust concerns that will not change. The resolution of those concerns — whether the supporting work either closes them out or exposes real gaps — is what would move me to higher confidence in a specific direction. A deferral of a few months, used well, is cheap insurance against a 30-year commitment.

## What would change my view

- If the stress-tested VfM still shows a clear P3 advantage and the integration register is credible, I would support P3 commitment with higher confidence.
- If PPP Canada signals the funding window will close during a short deferral, the deferral calculus changes and the board must weigh funding loss against diligence gain explicitly.
- If technical advisors assess cumulative integration risk as severe rather than manageable, I would shift toward conventional delivery regardless of VfM.
- If neighbourhood engagement reveals material legitimacy gaps on at-grade running, the alignment itself — not just the delivery model — needs revisiting before any commitment.
